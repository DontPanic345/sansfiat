# Sans Fiat — Game Design Document

Oct 17, 2026 · @Alan

## Vision and pillars

Sans Fiat ("without fiat money") is a turn-based hex 4X set at the dawn of farming. It has no treasury: nothing is bought with gold from the player's coffers, and a good is worth only what the people in the world will trade for it. The era runs from the last hunter-gatherers to the first cities, through the spread of crops and tamed animals.

The game keeps the genre's familiar loop of explore, expand, exploit and exterminate. It replaces the usual abstractions with simulated people, food and distance.

| Pillar | What it means in play | Where it was tested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No money | Goods are physical, sit in a place, spoil, and must be carried. Value comes from trade at markets, never from a price list. Anything that acts like money (shells, beads, clay tokens) is made by someone and traded. | hex-economy |
| People are the economy | Clans eat, grow, split, move and trade on their own. The player steers their own people and persuades others; nobody else is a puppet. | hex-economy |
| Distance is the cost | Every action spends labour, and labour drains with distance from food. Logistics, not hard caps, limits how far an empire or an army reaches. | supply-lines, scout |
| Ground is held by people | Territory is held only while someone stands on it or works it. Holding ground costs troops or settlers. | paint-army |
| The map is earned | The true cost of terrain is unknown until scouted, and routes improve as trails and bridges are built. Infrastructure is shared by everyone. | scout |
| One world, many starts | The map and its people are generated by a simulated pre-game, so every start sits in a history that explains it. | hex-economy |

The source material is the five sandbox prototypes in `D:\lab\sandbox` and the design notes in the SansFiat project folder. Where this document goes beyond them, it says so with the word **proposed**.

## Game structure

A game has three acts: a pre-game the player watches, a founding where it takes a civ, and the main game it plays. A turn stands for a full round of seasons, not a fixed number of years.

1. **Pre-game (simulated, about 150–200 turns).** A few seed clans spread over an empty map, hold land, use it up, split and trade at gatherings. The player watches it at high speed or skips it. It ends once the land is settled and there is one thriving market per player, at least 8 tiles apart, each with domesticated grain in its reach.
2. **Founding.** The best markets become the first civs; one goes to each player, human or AI. A civ's first people are the clans settled within its market's reach, and the market site is its first city. Other gatherings still running become permanent markets. All other clans stay unaligned and peaceful.
3. **Main game.** Players expand, explore, build, trade and fight. Unaligned clans keep living by the pre-game's rules, so the world keeps moving around the players.

### Turn order (main game)

One turn runs these steps in order. Steps 1–6 come from the hex-economy `TURN` pipeline; steps 7–9 are **proposed** additions for the civs.

1. Regrow: tiles regrow plants and fish; game breeds back.
2. Forage and work: clans and civ workers harvest, move, or adjust their land.
3. Trade: everyone in reach of a market posts a basket, and each market clears.
4. Settle: everyone eats; people grow, starve, split or die.
5. Occupy: held grain land tames further; land left alone goes feral.
6. Markets: gatherings form or fade; new markets appear where settlement is dense.
7. Player orders: each civ's queued actions resolve (move, paint, build, trail, found).
8. Armies: zones re-spread, contested tiles skirmish, units eat and resupply.
9. Discoveries and victory checks.

## Map and terrain

The map is a pointy-top axial hex grid, 44 × 28 in hex-economy. Terrain decides which foods a tile carries, how hard it is to cross, and how well it defends. Each prototype tuned terrain separately; the table below merges them.

| Terrain | Move cost (labour) | Wild food cap | Defence | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plains | 1 | game 3, grain 4 | 0 | Best grain land; where grain is first domesticated |
| Forest | 2 | game 3, fruit 4 | +25% | A trail can bring cost to 1 |
| Hills | 2–3 | game 2, fruit 1 | +50% | A trail can bring cost to 1–2 |
| Desert | 1 | none | 0 | Crossable, feeds no one |
| Mountain | impassable | none | — | Some have a pass a bridge or trail can open |
| Ocean | impassable | fish/shellfish when shallow | — | Shallows next to land: fish 4, shellfish 4 |

**Features** add food on top: a river adds fish 5 and grain 1; a coast adds shellfish 1. **Fertility** varies in patches from 0.4× to 2× and scales every food on a tile, so river valleys and coasts in rich patches stand out.

**Generation** combines both map generators: smoothed height noise, wandering mountain ridges with hilly flanks and rare passes (scout), then inlets, inland seas, deserts (about 20% of land), rivers and coastal shallows (hex-economy).

**Hidden cost (proposed for the whole map, from scout).** Forest, hill and mountain tiles hide the cost a trail would bring them to, and mountains hide whether a bridge can span them. A player sees the base cost until a scout reveals the tile.

**Fog of war (proposed).** Each player sees tiles its people, units or trading partners have seen. The pre-game's history is not revealed for free; a new civ knows only the land its founding clans held and walked.

## People and settlements

Every person on the map belongs to a clan, and a civ is a group of clans that act together. Cities are not built from nothing; they are clans that settled on rich land and stayed.

### Clans (from hex-economy)

- **Diet.** One food unit per person per turn. One food counts in full only up to 40% of need, and at 30% beyond it, so variety matters. Leftovers are stored, and 30% spoils each turn.
- **Land.** A clan holds a connected patch with its home at the centre, and forages it plus unclaimed neighbours. Its land's total move cost is capped at 6, about six plains or three forest tiles.
- **Fed state.** Fully fed: grows 3% a turn, rising to 7% after 30 settled turns. Fed 80–100%: holds steady. Below 80%: shrinks, and dies out under one person.
- **Dividing.** At 12 people a clan divides its land in two, and the daughter takes the far half. A starving clan of 8 sends a daughter to a site within 6 steps that would feed her.
- **Moving on.** After 3 starving turns in a row a clan walks to better ground within 4 steps.
- **Tended grain.** Grain held 15 turns in a row starts to tame. About 120 turns later it is domesticated at 2.5× wild yield. Left alone, it goes feral at the same pace.

### Civ settlements (proposed)

- **City.** A civ's clan that has settled on domesticated land becomes a city. Its population is the clans living on its land. Its work radius is 1 and territory radius 2, as in hex4x.
- **Expansion replaces the settler.** A civ grows when its own clans divide. The player chooses where a daughter goes (any site within reach that would feed her), instead of building a settler unit. Founding needs the daughter's people and a food store for the walk; it costs no gold.
- **Minimum spacing.** New cities sit at least 3 tiles from any other city.
- **Joining.** An unaligned clan may join a civ that it trades with heavily, or one whose grain it needs. Joining is the peaceful route to growth and is the core of diplomacy with clans (see Standard 4X elements).
- **Unaligned clans stay peaceful.** They cannot be attacked in the proof of concept; pushing them off land happens only by out-competing them for it.

## Economy without money

The player never holds a balance. Everything a civ has is a physical good in a place: food in a store, flint at a quarry, pots in a city. A good's value is the exchange ratio the last market cleared at, and that ratio is nobody's property.

### Markets (from hex-economy)

- Each trader posts its whole basket. It values a basket at the sum of the square roots of its amounts, so a varied basket beats a large single one.

```latex
U(\text{basket}) = \sum_f \sqrt{x_f}
```

- The market searches for exchange ratios at which supply meets demand (up to 300 iterations), then rations whichever side is long so goods are conserved exactly. Ratios are relative only, with a geometric mean of 1.
- Trading is value-neutral at the clearing ratios, and everyone leaves better off because their basket is more varied.
- A clan goes to market only if the gain beats the trip. The trip costs 5% of a turn's labour per unit of path cost, and a market reaches 3 path cost.
- **Gatherings** are temporary pre-game markets. One forms where 4 or more clans are in reach, and fades when its traded volume drops below 2.

### Goods (proposed expansion)

The prototypes trade five foods. The proof of concept adds materials and crafts so that building has something to consume.

| Kind | Goods | Comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food | game, grain, fruit, fish, shellfish | Foraging and farming (prototyped) |
| Material | wood, stone, flint, clay, hides | Forest, hills, rivers; hides from game |
| Craft | tools, pottery, cloth | Made in a city from materials plus labour |
| Token | shell beads | Made from shellfish shells; valued only because clans want them |

Tokens test the second rule of the vision: if beads come to act like money, it is because clans trade for them, not because the game says so.

### Production (proposed)

- **Labour is the cost of everything.** A civ's people have labour each turn. Fed people give full labour; hungry people give less. Food surplus therefore *is* production capacity.
- **A project costs labour plus goods.** A granary might cost 20 labour, 6 wood and 4 stone. Goods must be in the city's store or delivered along a route.
- **The player gets goods by trading surplus.** The city's store goes to market like any clan's basket. There is no price list; the player sees last turn's ratios and decides what to post.
- **City supply funds armies.** Food that feeds troops is food the city can't trade or turn into labour, as in supply-lines (a big army slows building at home).

## Supply and logistics

Armies run on food, and food comes from home. The further a unit is from a city, the more of its labour goes to staying fed, so reach is set by logistics, not by a hard rule. Everything below comes from supply-lines unless marked proposed.

- **Labour.** Each unit has a labour pool, 4 for a warrior, refreshed each turn. Moving, fighting, resupplying, foraging and pillaging all draw on it.
- **Food store.** A warrior carries 4 food. It eats 1 a turn and 1 more per fight. With an empty store it can't attack and loses 3 strength a turn; when fed it heals 1 a turn.
- **Supply line.** A unit refills from the nearest city with spare food. The line runs through friendly or neutral land; it can reach into enemy land but not pass through it. Length is summed terrain cost, capped at 10.
- **Line cost.** A resupply costs 1 labour plus 1 per 3 cost of line. Near home it's nearly free; at the edge of range it takes the whole turn.
- **Forage** (2 labour, own or neutral land) and **pillage** (1 labour, enemy land) take food from the tile. The tile then yields nothing to anyone for 5 turns.
- **Visible lines.** A selected unit shows its supply line on the map, and whether it can draw on it this turn.
- **Automation (proposed).** Two toggles per unit: auto-supply tops up along the line, and auto-forage feeds off the land when cut off. Each keeps a reserve of labour the player sets, so a unit can still move or fight.
- **Lines carry goods too (proposed).** The same routing moves materials to a building project, so a quarry far from the city costs labour each turn to use.
- **Captains share one store (proposed).** In combination with the paint-army system, a captain's army draws as one unit along one line. Detachments far out in the zone add their distance.

### Porters and supply caches

A supply line from the city works near home. Far campaigns need porters carrying food forward to a cache.

- **Supply cache.** A store of food and goods on a tile, set up by a porter or held in a fort. It spoils at the normal 30% a turn unless a granary-type discovery improves it. An enemy that takes the tile takes the cache.
- **Porter.** A non-combat unit that carries food from a city or market to a cache, or straight to an army. It shares a tile with any other unit, friendly troops included, and doesn't count as holding ground.
- **Porters eat what they carry.** A porter spends labour on the route and eats from its load each turn. The longer the trip, the less arrives, which is the classic limit on how far armies can campaign.
- **Standing orders.** A porter can be given a city and a cache, and keeps the cache stocked to a level the player sets.
- **Armies draw from the nearest source.** A captain resupplies from whichever is cheaper to reach: a city with spare food, or a friendly cache.
- **Pack animals (later).** Herding could give porters a larger load, which makes it a military discovery as well as a food one.

## Exploration and infrastructure

Scouting finds the cheapest way through the land, and trails make that way cheaper for everyone. Because supply lines and market reach are measured in path cost, a trail extends an army's reach and a market's catchment at once. The scout prototype has no design note; this section is drawn from its code.

- **Hidden cost.** At map generation every forest, hill and mountain tile rolls the cost a trail would bring it to. Forest goes to 1 or 2; hills to 1 or 2 (2 is twice as likely). Mountains go to 3 if bridgeable: a span of 1 or 2, or (8 times out of 10) never passable.
- **Scout routes.** The player gives a scout a route from A to B. It plans an optimistic path (unknown tiles at their best case) and a known one (unknown tiles at their worst case). It reveals unknown tiles on the optimistic path, 1 labour each, until the optimistic path can't beat the known one.
- **Trails.** Then the scout builds trails along the best known route: 2 labour for forest, 3 for hills, 6 for a mountain pass. A bridge in the prototype spans 1.
- **Shared infrastructure.** Trails sit on the tile, so every player, clan and army uses them. Building a trail toward a rival opens your door too.
- **Map memory (proposed).** Revealed costs belong to the player who scouted them. They can be learned at a market from the clans who trade there, so contact spreads knowledge.

The scout replaces hex4x's generic warrior-explorer. In the 4X frame it is the explore pillar, but its output is cheaper routes, not just uncovered fog.

## Military

Armies hold ground by standing on it. A **captain** is the one visible unit and carries an army of troops. The player paints a zone, and the army spreads over it and holds it. This comes from the paint-army prototypes and the Captain and Territory note.

- **Zone.** A connected chain of painted tiles belonging to a captain. Painting only adds tiles and unpainting only removes them; the zone is never redrawn.
- **The captain moves on his own.** Moving the captain doesn't move the zone or the troops deployed on it. A deployed captain who steps onto an unpainted tile paints it, so walking out from the zone extends it as a trail behind him.
- **Spread.** At end of turn the army splits evenly over the zone and creeps out one tile a turn. Losing troops or changing the zone triggers a re-spread. Dug-in troops stay put.
- **Deployed and undeployed.** With a zone, the army is spread out. With an empty zone, every troop gathers on the captain and marches with him, and his steps paint nothing. To advance, unpaint the rear and paint the front.
- **Cut-off detachments.** A detachment that loses its link to its captain may promote one of its own to captain, for free, and becomes a new army. Otherwise it keeps moving toward its captain and rejoins when the two meet.
- **Territory is held, not owned.** A tile is yours only while a troop stands on it, a fort holds it, or it's within a city's territory radius. When the last troop leaves, it reverts to neutral.
- **Skirmish.** A tile painted by both sides fights only between the troops on that tile, inflicting half of each side's power per round. There is no all-out battle.
- **Entrenchment.** Troops that stay put gain 25% defence a turn, up to 3 turns (+75%).
- **Retreat.** A defender outnumbered 3 to 1 has a 60% chance to slip away and survive. A bunched army can take thinly held tiles cheaply, but rarely kills a picket.
- **Forts.** Troops can spend labour, wood and stone to build a fort on a tile. A fort starts at full entrenchment, holds its tile with a small garrison, and can hold a supply cache (see Supply and logistics).
- **Raising troops (proposed).** Troops are people. A city raises them from its clans, which takes them out of the labour force and puts them on the city's food supply. Prototype values: armies of 10, 3 in a city garrison, at most 3 captains.
- **Supply.** Detachments eat from the army's store, which refills from the nearest supply cache or city (see Supply and logistics). A thin zone far from a cache costs more to feed than a compact one.
- **Cities.** Taking an enemy city means holding its tile. A city gets +50% defence. The city's clans don't vanish: they stay on the land and may join the new owner.

Warriors from hex4x and supply-lines become troops; the captain is the unit the player moves.

## Standard 4X elements

All of the genre's usual parts are in, but each is rebuilt on people, goods and distance. Everything in this section is **proposed** except where a prototype is named.

| 4X element | Usual form | In Sans Fiat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explore | Scout reveals fog | Scout reveals hidden cost and builds shared trails (scout) |
| Expand | Build a settler, found a city | A civ's clan divides; the player picks where the daughter settles. Unaligned clans can join. |
| Exploit | Tiles yield food, production and gold | Tiles yield foods and materials; fed people give labour; surplus is traded at markets (hex-economy) |
| Exterminate | Units attack units | Captains paint zones; skirmish per tile; supply limits reach (paint-army, supply-lines) |
| Production queue | Spend production on an item | Spend labour and delivered goods on a project |
| Buildings | Bought with production or gold | Granary (less spoilage), workshop (crafts), shrine (gathering draw), wall (defence), storehouse (larger store) |
| Technology | Spend science on a tree | Discoveries come from practice (below) |
| Diplomacy | Treaties and gold deals | Trade at shared markets, joining clans, truces, and access for supply lines |
| AI rivals | Scripted opponents | AI civs use the same actions as the player (hex4x `ai.js` as base) |
| Victory | Conquest, score, science | Conquest, or prosperity (below) |

### Discoveries

There is no research points pool. A discovery comes from doing the thing, the same way grain is domesticated by holding grain land.

- **Agriculture** (prototyped): domesticated grain from 135 turns of tending. Every civ starts with it.
- **Herding:** hunting a game tile lightly for many turns tames herds; game becomes a renewable food that can move with its people.
- **Pottery:** working clay near a river; lowers spoilage.
- **Weaving:** hides plus fibre; makes cloth, a high-value trade good.
- **Stoneworking:** quarrying hills; unlocks walls and better tools.
- **Spread by contact:** a discovery spreads to clans and civs that trade at the same market, slowly. Trade is how knowledge travels.

### Diplomacy

- **With clans:** clans don't sign treaties. A clan joins the civ it depends on most, measured by its share of goods traded with that civ.
- **With civs:** truce (no painting on each other's land), passage (supply lines may cross the other's land), and shared market (both may post there).
- **No tribute in gold.** A deal moves goods, land or passage, never a currency.

### Victory

- **Conquest:** hold every rival's founding city.
- **Prosperity:** after a set turn limit, the civ with the most people wins. People are the measure because nothing else is counted in coin.

## Proof-of-concept scope

The proof of concept is one playable game: a simulated pre-game, then a human civ against 1–3 AI civs, played to a conquest or prosperity win on a 44 × 28 map. It should answer one question: is a 4X with no treasury fun to steer?

### In

- Pre-game from hex-economy, run headless at game start with a fast-forward view
- Founding: the best markets become civs, and other gatherings become permanent markets
- Civ clans, cities, expansion by division, clans joining
- Markets with foods plus 3 materials (wood, stone, clay) and 1 craft (pottery)
- Labour-and-goods projects: granary, storehouse, wall
- Captains, painted zones, skirmish, entrenchment, retreat
- Supply lines, forage and pillage, auto-supply toggles, porters, supply caches and forts
- Scouts, hidden cost, trails
- Two discoveries beyond agriculture: herding and pottery
- Fog of war; conquest and prosperity victories
- Simple AI civs on the same action set

### What makes it sing

Four additions, all in scope, that turn the simulation into a game the player can read and steer.

1. **Show why things happen.** Click a clan to see why it is starving, why it moved, or why it joined a civ. Click a market to see which goods moved its ratios and who traded. Each rule records a short reason when it acts, and the side panel shows it.
2. **Good and bad years.** Each turn every region of the map gets a yield multiplier: usually near 1, sometimes a drought (0.5) or a bumper year (1.5), lasting a few turns. Stores, granaries, trade and porters matter because next year is uncertain.
3. **Clans are the prize.** Each unaligned clan has a leaning toward each civ, built from trade with that civ's markets and from food given in hard years. A civ can gift food to a starving clan. A clan whose leaning passes a threshold joins. The map shows leanings, so rivals compete for clans openly.
4. **Pick your start.** When the pre-game ends, the player sees the founding markets and chooses which one to lead. AI civs take the rest.

### Out (for later)

- Shell-bead tokens, cloth, and most of the discovery list
- Civ-to-civ treaties beyond truce
- Attacking unaligned clans
- Rivers and sea as transport
- Save/load beyond the seed-plus-action replay the base already has

### Architecture (carried from the prototypes)

- Plain ES modules, no build step, no dependencies; `npm start`, `npm test` (node --test), `npm run sim`.
- `rules.js` holds all logic: `dispatch(state, action) -> {state, error?}` is pure. Actions have `validate`, `apply` and `candidates`, so the AI and tests use the same entry point as the UI.
- State is plain JSON. All randomness goes through `rand(state)`, so a seed plus the action list replays exactly.
- Only `render.js` and `main.js` touch the DOM. Debug map views from hex-economy carry over.
- The new game lives in `~/sansfiat`, under git from day one. hex-economy is the base, because it is the largest and has the turn pipeline; the other prototypes are merged into it as modules (`supply.js`, `army.js`, `scout.js`).

### Milestones

1. **Base:** hex-economy copied into the repo; merged map generator with hidden trail costs; tests green.
2. **Founding:** pre-game ends in civs; a city view; the player sees their clans and store.
3. **Economy:** materials, projects, civ trading at markets, expansion by division.
4. **Exploration:** scouts, trails, fog of war.
5. **War:** captains and zones, skirmish, supply lines, city capture.
6. **Rivals and victory:** AI civs, discoveries, victory screens, a full game played to the end.

Each milestone ends playable in the browser and with `npm run sim` producing a full game on a fixed seed.

## Open questions and risks

The biggest risk is that a game with no balance to spend feels like watching, not steering. The proof of concept should make the player's levers obvious: where daughters settle, what to post at market, what to build, where to paint.

### Carried from the design notes

- [ ] About 20% of clans are starving at game start. Is that the right pressure?
- [ ] Should grain tame only while its holder is growing, so domestication comes later and less evenly?
- [ ] Should daughters trade on better terms with parent clans, a kinship network?
- [ ] Should domesticated grain be required near a founding market, or is a busy market enough?
- [ ] How strong should entrenchment and retreat be?
- [ ] Can enemy units cut a supply line or attack porters, or only enemy territory?
- [ ] How do auto-supply and auto-forage share labour the player wants for moving or fighting?
- [ ] What links a detachment to its captain: a distance, a connected zone, or a supply line?

### New with the combined design

- [ ] **Performance.** The market clears with up to 300 iterations, for each of 20–30 markets, every turn. Fine headless; needs checking with a UI and 4 civs.
- [ ] **Pre-game length.** At 150–200 turns it must run in a few seconds, or be cached per seed.
- [ ] **Labour as the only cost.** Does labour plus goods give enough meaningful choices, or does it collapse into "more food is always right"?
- [ ] **Joining rule.** Is "depends on you most for trade" a lever the player can actually pull?
- [ ] **Prosperity victory.** Does counting people reward turtling? A share of all people trading at your markets may be a better measure.
- [ ] **Unit scale.** Troops (10 a captain) and clans (6–12 people) must use the same idea of a person, or raising troops makes no sense.
