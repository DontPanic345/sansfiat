# Vision

Sans Fiat (working title) means "without fiat money". It's an economy simulation with a game
layer on top, set at the time of the first civilisations: the spread of agriculture and animal
domestication.

## No abstractions

Strategy games make player interaction easy with abstractions. The best known is the gold coin
in the corner of the screen: gathered from the land somehow, saved up, then spent all at once
on a building. It makes sense and it's easy to play, and it's the one Sans Fiat challenges.

1. There is no money or gold in the player's coffers that lets them buy things or interact with
   the game.
2. A resource is worth something only because the people (actors) in the game want it. If
   something does the job of money (shells, beads, markings on clay balls and so on), an actor
   produces it and it's traded. Its value comes from what other actors will give for it.
3. Nothing is free. Every unit, building and action costs real goods and real people's time,
   taken from somewhere in the world.
4. Every abstraction has to justify itself. Before any abstraction goes into the game, we ask
   what real thing it stands for and whether the game needs the shortcut. If it can't answer
   both, it doesn't go in.

## A world that runs without you

The economy is a simulation that runs on its own. People forage, farm, eat, grow, move and
trade whether or not the player is watching. The depth is in the model behind the scenes, much
of which the player never sees directly. The world feels big because it is big.

The player gets a few touch points into that world. They don't control the economy; it emerges.
Much of the game's tension comes from that lack of control.

## The player

The game opens on a boon year. The harvest is far bigger than anyone needs, and the surplus
comes to the market where your house stands. You're the organiser of the biggest clan around,
and for whatever reason it falls to you to decide what to do with the surplus. That surplus is
the start of your civ.

From there, the player:

- controls an army,
- controls their territory,
- defends their supply lines.

How to get anything done without a treasury is the problem the player spends the game solving,
not one the design solves for them.

## Winning

Not decided yet. These are the usual options, held equally open:

- **Conquest:** the last civ standing wins.
- **Time limit:** after a set number of turns, the civ with the most people, land or goods wins.
- **Influence:** most of the world's people choose to join your civ.
- **Great work:** you are the first to finish a project that takes the world's labour, such as a
  temple, a city wall or a monument.
- **Trade:** your market becomes the one the world trades at.

## Making it a game

The simulation comes first. The game layer is what makes it playable, and it is held to the
same rule: nothing is free, and every abstraction has to justify itself. It needs:

- a way to win and a way to lose,
- a start screen, a new game, saving and loading,
- feedback when things happen: what changed, why, and where,
- a way for a new player to learn the few touch points they have.

## What it isn't

- It doesn't borrow its feel from other strategy games. Its point of difference has to come
  from the game itself.
- It isn't a game of building everything at once. It grows slowly, one mechanic at a time,
  each kept only if it earns its place.
